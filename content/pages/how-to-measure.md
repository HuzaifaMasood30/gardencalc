# How to Measure for a Materials Estimate

Every quantity on this site comes down to three numbers: length, width and depth. Get
those right and the calculator does the rest. Get them wrong and the answer is wrong by
the same proportion. This guide covers how to measure a space, how to break an awkward
shape into rectangles, and how much to add for waste.

## Start with a plan of the space

Sketch the area on paper before you measure anything. Mark the walls, the doorways, the
obstacles and anything you are not covering. A rough sketch takes two minutes and stops
you measuring the same edge twice or forgetting a section entirely.

Note the units on the sketch as you go. Mixing feet and inches is the most common source
of a wrong order, and it is easy to do when you are moving between rooms.

## Measuring rectangular areas

Measure the length and the width of the space, then multiply them to get square feet. Take
each measurement twice, once from each end, and use the larger figure if they differ. Rooms
are rarely perfectly square, and an order placed on the smaller figure can come up short.

For a floor, measure wall to wall at the widest point. For a wall, measure the length and
the ceiling height, then subtract the area of doors and windows before you work out paint.
For a bed, measure the planted area, not the whole garden.

## Breaking up irregular shapes

Most real spaces are not one rectangle. Split the area into the rectangles it is made of,
work out each one, and add them together. An L-shaped lawn becomes two rectangles; a border
that curves becomes a series of straight sections.

For a circle, measure the diameter and multiply it by itself, then by 0.785. For a triangle,
multiply the base by the height and halve it. If a shape is genuinely irregular, measure the
straightest approximation you can and add a little more waste to cover the difference.

## Adding waste and overage

Almost no material is bought exactly to the calculated figure. Cuts, breakage, spillage and
settling all take a share. The table below shows the allowances we use across the
calculators on this site.

| Material | Typical waste | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mulch, bark, compost | 5% | Settles after spreading |
| Topsoil, soil | 10% | Compacts and settles |
| Gravel, sand | 10% | Spreading loss and uneven depth |
| Concrete | 10% | Spillage and uneven form depth |
| Tiles, straight layout | 10% | Edge and corner cuts |
| Tiles, diagonal layout | 15% | More cuts at every angle |
| Sod | 5 to 10% | Trimming around edges |
| Paint | One extra litre | Touch-ups and a second coat |

Round up to the next whole bag, box or pallet once you have added the waste. Half a bag is
not something a supplier will sell you, and running out mid-job costs more in time than the
spare material costs in money.

## Converting between units

Suppliers quote in different units, so it helps to move between them quickly. These are the
conversions the calculators apply.

| From | To | Multiply by |
|---|---|---|
| Square feet | Square metres | 0.093 |
| Cubic feet | Cubic yards | 0.037 |
| Cubic yards | Cubic feet | 27 |
| Cubic yards | Litres | 764.6 |
| Inches | Centimetres | 2.54 |
| Feet | Metres | 0.305 |

A cubic yard is 27 cubic feet, which is why a bulk order measured in yards sounds much
smaller than the same volume in feet. A yard of mulch spread three inches deep covers about
108 square feet.

## Common measuring mistakes

- Using the outside of a raised bed instead of the inside, which over-buys soil for the timber.
- Measuring one end of a room and assuming the other matches.
- Forgetting to subtract doors and windows before working out paint.
- Pacing out a distance and treating it as exact.
- Ordering the calculated figure with no waste allowance, then making a second trip.
- Mixing feet and inches in the same calculation.
